\begin{abstract}

Biomedical studies often report the distributions and associations needed for a new analysis while access to individual records remains limited. We study whether a scientific agent can use this evidence to generate an entire cohort on which researchers can run their intended analyses. The agent identifies what each study measured, translates reported statistics into parameters of a joint model, and records assumptions for relationships the studies leave open. We first tested the workflow where the source model was available. In a longitudinal memory analysis, the agent and a reconstruction of the published generator both recovered a negative interaction below the HbA1c threshold, while their mean estimates for the weaker upper-branch interaction differed in sign. We then evaluated generation from external studies of glycemia and lipids. Six agent constructions produced 18 tables. All six requested quantities agreed in point direction with sample references across the draws, yet the three construction means for residual lipid correlation ranged from 0.108 to 0.141, compared with 0.512 in the reference sample. In a separate evidence comparison, adding published relationships reduced mean absolute discrepancy for five of six regression coefficients. In an earlier prospective glucose study, all 15 agent draws recovered the directions of the BMI and age associations, while eight recovered the sex difference in the BMI slope. Three of five constructions met the joint accuracy criterion, below the four required. Partial evidence can support specific analyses while leaving other relationships unresolved.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A scientific question may be ready before the necessary biomedical data are available. A cohort may not have measured a biomarker, individual records from another study may be inaccessible, or no accessible dataset may contain the requested combination of variables. Yet relevant evidence can exist in published means, distributions, adjusted coefficients and subgroup findings. Turning those findings into a complete virtual table would let researchers execute the intended analysis, examine the implications of the evidence and identify what additional information matters. The desired product is scientific data accompanied by an explicit account of how they were constructed.


The difficult step is forming a joint law from heterogeneous evidence. A marginal correlation and an adjusted regression coefficient describe different quantities. Findings also depend on eligibility, measurement, population and adjustment sets. Two correctly extracted coefficients cannot generally be inserted unchanged into one joint equation, and the dependence needed to connect them may be unreported. Consequently, evidence-based generation requires scientific modeling decisions beyond document retrieval and row sampling. Those decisions determine which analyses the resulting dataset can support.


We investigate a workflow in which a scientific agent makes these decisions. The researcher supplies the population, variables, available observations and intended analyses. The agent acquires or receives studies, determines their numerical roles, authors a joint generating law and inspects the resulting data. Numerical tools perform calculations, sampling and analysis; reviews check the statistical meaning and implementation of the specification. The scientific question remains visible to the agent throughout construction. Complete-cohort generation is central: the main experiments supply no individual construction records, and the agent generates every requested variable.


This division of work moves task-specific equations, distribution choices and parameter derivations into the agent's construction process. It also leaves a concrete object to inspect. A researcher can trace a generated association back to a reported statistic, a mathematical derivation or an explicitly assumed relationship. When an assumption changes a scientific conclusion, the workflow can expose the missing evidence instead of hiding it inside a completed table. The approach uses established probability families, allowing the model to change with the request and available evidence.


We formulate cohort construction around a scientific request: the target population, available measurements, admissible studies and proposed analysis. Our method assigns a numerical role to each finding, constructs a joint model, and exposes the assumptions needed to sample complete records. We evaluate the resulting data through the scientific analyses for which they were generated, including adjusted associations, repeated-measures interactions and residual dependence.


We begin with a case in which the generating model is available. In the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) example, the agent and a reconstruction of the published generator receive the same model exports and their simulated cohorts undergo the same longitudinal analysis. The glycemia and lipid studies pose a different problem: they supply evidence from external populations, and the agent must choose how to connect the variables in a joint law. A separate comparison varies which published relationships are supplied. We also report an earlier, prospectively frozen test of the underlying approach. Its target measurements were revealed after all constructions closed. This separates reuse of known references during development from evaluation on a previously withheld sample.


\section{Related work and problem definition}

\subsection{From available information to analyzable records}

Filshtein et al. share fitted study models so a recipient can simulate complete cohorts for epidemiological analyses [1]. SynthIPD uses published survival curves and trial summaries to reconstruct time-to-event records with covariates [2]. SynDI combines internal individual data with external regression information across populations to fit a target regression [29]. Multilevel network meta-regression likewise relates aggregate evidence to individual-level models under population assumptions [3]. These approaches differ in what the recipient knows. Filshtein et al. supply the comparison for our HRS experiment because the published exports and the scientific analysis can be matched. In our external-evidence experiments, the agent must also choose the joint dependence structure needed to perform the requested analyses.


G-Sim combines LLM-authored simulators with numerical calibration and structural refinement [4]. LLMSynthor uses aggregate discrepancies to guide proposals for new records and evaluates mobility, e-commerce and household population synthesis [5]. SynthCraft studies healthcare synthetic-data workflows [6], and SPADA samples from a dependency graph fitted with observed tabular data [15]. Our methodological question concerns the preceding biomedical decision: how findings with different populations, scales and adjustment sets constrain one generator, and which analyses the resulting data support.


\subsection{Evidence-conditioned cohort construction}

A request specifies the target population, eligibility and measurements, desired variables, permitted observations and intended analyses. Each evidence record identifies its source population, measurement scale, statistical operator, adjustment set, estimate, available uncertainty and provenance. The agent returns an executable generator, the numerical uses of that evidence and the assumptions needed to complete the model:


\noindent\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}

\begin{lstlisting}

(generator, evidence uses, completion assumptions)

    = agent(scientific request, evidence, optional observations)

virtual data = generator(number of rows, random seed)

scientific results = requested analyses(virtual data).

\end{lstlisting}

\end{minipage}

In whole-cohort construction, optional observations are empty and all requested variables are generated. In enrichment, supplied covariates remain fixed while an unmeasured variable is generated. These modes share the evidence-to-law problem; the principal experiments here concern complete cohorts.


A finding may constrain the law numerically, provide a check on its implications, or remain contextual. Algebraic consequences of accepted findings are derived evidence. Unreported dependence and transfer from a source population to the request are completion assumptions. Several laws can satisfy the available information while implying different conditional results, so the output includes both the table and its construction specification.


We evaluate usefulness at the level of scientific analysis. For an adjusted coefficient, correlation or dispersion statistic, the generated estimate is compared with the relevant sample reference in its native units. Reproducing a supplied summary measures reconstruction; agreement with a target result not supplied to the author addresses a different question. A table can support several analyses with different degrees of evidential support. These distinctions guide both construction and evaluation.

\section{Agent-controlled evidence-to-cohort construction}

\subsection{Qualify evidence against the scientific request}

The workflow begins with the population, era, measurements, units, eligibility and scientific analyses. It accepts curated evidence and agent-directed acquisition. With retrieval enabled, the agent selects queries and primary studies to address evidence gaps; the retained record distinguishes full text, partial access and bibliographic metadata and records overlap or exclusions. Researchers supply the request and access conditions, while the agent chooses how the admitted evidence enters the model.


Qualification preserves each finding's original statistical meaning. Logistic disease coefficients cannot become continuous-biomarker slopes, grouped contrasts cannot become per-unit effects, and rank correlations require different treatment from Pearson correlations. Source and requested adjustment sets must also be reconciled. Unit conversion supplies an algebraic transformation rather than evidence of population or assay equivalence. A finding with unresolved definitions can remain useful context without being promoted to a numerical constraint.


In the external-evidence study, researchers supplied audited source packets and measurement guidance [7–\allowbreak{}11]. Other implementations combined reviewed articles with optional retrieval or allowed authors to select their own sources through a bounded Europe PMC/\allowbreak{}PMC gateway. The host dispatched authors and coordinated access, review and execution. These implementations share the scientific decision sequence while preserving their separate evidence budgets and input conditions.


\subsection{Translate findings into numerical model constraints}

The agent authors a model specification, generator code, evidence-use records and a decision explanation. A relationship counts as numerical evidence use when its role is visible in the implemented law or in an explicit calculation. The common evidence-use record connects relationship, dispersion, scenario, check-only and retained-assumption decisions to sources and specification locations. It distinguishes arithmetic from geometric summaries, marginal from conditional effects, rank from Pearson dependence, and individual variation from uncertainty in an estimate.


A worked example shows why this step requires modeling. Jansen et al. report separate age/\allowbreak{}sex-adjusted HbA1c projections for BMI and glucose [9]. For residualized BMI and glucose with SDs \texttt{sB} and \texttt{sG} and correlation \texttt{r}, a joint response law with coefficients \texttt{thetaB} and \texttt{thetaG} implies:


\noindent\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}

\begin{lstlisting}

cB = thetaB + thetaG * r * sG/sB

cG = thetaG + thetaB * r * sB/sG.

\end{lstlisting}

\end{minipage}

CHNS E1 constructor A01, selected by ordinal identifier, used reported slopes \texttt{cB = 0.13} and \texttt{cG = 2.29}, residual SDs 4.07611 and 0.49694, and the declared assumption \texttt{r = 0.25}. Solving the equations gave 0.0642173 and 2.1583154 in IFCC response units. Multiplication by 0.09148 yielded 0.00587460 and 0.19744269 in HbA1c percentage-point units [11]. The source projections therefore informed the joint law through their mathematical relationship, with residual dependence and population transfer remaining explicit assumptions. The worked trace connects the derivation to frozen code, an unfitted correlation check and the resulting target BMI discrepancy.


\subsection{Complete an executable joint law}

The agent chooses the probability family, dependencies, parameters and remaining completion assumptions. These choices must support complete rows and the requested scientific analyses. Means and SDs alone do not determine residual dependence, tails or subgroup structure, so the specification records how those properties were supplied. Reviews inspect source meaning, numerical derivations, specification/\allowbreak{}code consistency and required finite moments before execution.


Figure 1 presents a completed construction using a sex-stratified Gaussian copula and positive biomarker margins. The agent converted a reported glucose–\allowbreak{}HbA1c Spearman relationship into a latent Gaussian correlation, declared the missing BMI–\allowbreak{}glucose dependence and implemented sampling and measurement transforms. Other requests led to bounded conditional laws, gamma innovations, lognormal mixtures or a recursive longitudinal family. The scientific contribution lies in choosing and connecting these components to the evidence, rather than introducing a new distribution family.


\subsection{Execute and inspect the resulting analyses}

Deterministic tools execute the authored program through an isolated WSL/\allowbreak{}bubblewrap adapter and return allowed source and generated-data diagnostics. Semantic checks retain the declared measurement, operator and role; unsupported comparisons remain unavailable. The author records whether to retain or revise the model within the task budget. Candidate versions, final seeds and evaluation contracts are fixed before the corresponding execution or comparison.


Researchers receive the complete tables and their scientific specification, then apply ordinary regression, correlation or longitudinal-analysis software. This separates model authorship from reproducible numerical execution while keeping the analysis request central to both. Independent numerical implementations check final statistics and reference mappings. The workflow reduces the need for researchers to hand-author each generator equation, although human preparation-time savings were not measured.

\subsection{A shared longitudinal model tests reconstruction}

Filshtein et al. distributed fitted HRS model parameters so other researchers could simulate data for longitudinal analysis [1]. We supplied those exports and method code to the agent and separately reconstructed the published recipient-stage generator. Each law produced three complete tables with 11,509 synthetic subjects observed at four visits. We fitted the same mixed model of memory in every table. Its two prespecified interactions estimate how the age slope of standardized memory, per decade, changes per percentage point of HbA1c on each side of the 6.5\% spline knot.


Below the knot, the published coefficient was −0.04684. The agent and reconstructed-law estimates averaged −0.04464 and −0.04050 respectively, with all six draws negative (Figure 2). On the upper branch, the published coefficient was +0.01287. All three agent estimates were positive (mean +0.02649) and all three reconstructed-law estimates were negative (mean −0.01169). Five of the six upper-branch model-conditional intervals included zero. Agreement on a slope did not guarantee agreement on individual variation. The agent's upper-group HbA1c SD was 0.563 percentage points, close to the published synthetic summary of 0.6 but lower than the published original-sample SD of 1.4.


The comparison uses six valid tables. Our first three comparator attempts were invalid because our parser misread seven HbA1c coefficient and standard-error pairs. We corrected the parser inputs and reran only the affected comparator draws. The supplement retains all nine attempts. This benchmark measures preservation of supplied model information. The next study asks what can be recovered when the joint model itself must be inferred from the literature.

\subsection{External studies constrain some glycemic and lipid analyses}

The cross-task study used the common evidence-use interface for HbA1c and TC/\allowbreak{}HDL requests. All six agent laws completed an initial trial and three final draws, producing 18 distinct 3,000-row tables, or 54,000 fictional rows. H01–\allowbreak{}H03 chose different bounded conditional laws and HbA1c innovations. L01–\allowbreak{}L03 combined Gaussian copulas and lognormal margins with differing uses of geometric summaries, arithmetic moments, coefficients of variation and age dependence [25–\allowbreak{}28]. These choices were authored by the agents, with their derivations and assumptions recorded before execution.


All 54 primary point estimates agreed in direction with their corresponding cached sample estimates (Figure 3; Table S5), while magnitudes varied. HbA1c age effects averaged 0.09954–\allowbreak{}0.10091 percentage points per decade versus 0.07513 in the reference. Age/\allowbreak{}sex-adjusted BMI effects averaged 0.04892–\allowbreak{}0.06030 versus 0.08364 per 5 kg/\allowbreak{}m². After adding glucose, H03 averaged 0.05098 versus 0.05664, whereas H01/\allowbreak{}H02 averaged 0.03047/\allowbreak{}0.01936. The tables therefore supported the intended regressions and retained the attenuation pattern after glucose adjustment, with unequal quantitative agreement.


Lipid results made the distinction between separate effects and joint dependence especially clear. HDL BMI effects averaged −2.1865 to −1.9868 mg/\allowbreak{}dL per 5 kg/\allowbreak{}m² versus −4.6230; TC effects averaged 10.0151–\allowbreak{}11.2609 versus 5.6831. The TC reference sensitivity interval crossed zero, so its positive sample point is not an established positive population direction. Across constructors, mean adjusted residual TC–\allowbreak{}HDL correlations ranged from 0.10828 to 0.14134 versus 0.51207. The laws reproduced focal point directions while substantially underrepresenting this remaining joint association.


Dispersion further distinguished the analyses these tables might support. H03's relatively close glucose-adjusted BMI effect coexisted with HbA1c marginal SD/\allowbreak{}residual RMS of 0.3523/\allowbreak{}0.3006 versus 0.5274/\allowbreak{}0.4885. H02 retained more spread but smaller BMI effects. L02's TC SD was close to the reference, 43.6778 versus 42.8466 mg/\allowbreak{}dL, while its residual lipid correlation remained weak. No law uniformly dominated focal effects, dependence and dispersion. Independent recomputation verified all 513 prespecified estimates and diagnostics, including 324 matched cached-reference comparisons; 189 diagnostics lacked matching references and remained generated-only results.


\subsection{Scientific use before disclosure of the measured reference}

The earlier prospective study defined three analyses of capillary glucose in HAALSI adults aged 40 or older [30,31]. All five agent constructions, each sampled three times, recovered the positive directions of the adjusted BMI and age effects in the 4,394-person sample. The measured difference between female and male BMI slopes was −0.244 mmol/\allowbreak{}L per 5 kg/\allowbreak{}m². Only eight of 15 draws recovered its negative direction (Table S7, Figure S5).


The prespecified rule required four of five constructions to satisfy the margin for every analysis on every seed. Three passed in HAALSI and none in the companion Turkana evaluation [32]. In HAALSI, setting all three effects to zero also satisfied the 0.10 outcome-SD margin. This margin alone could not establish recovery of a nonzero relationship. The planned expansion stopped. The later Turkana analysis in Section 5.2 reused the same measured sample.

\subsection{Relational evidence improves analysis agreement}

All 24 Q1 constructions produced their three final tables, yielding 72 distinct tables and all 216 focal coefficient estimates. Additional relational evidence reduced constructor-averaged mean absolute discrepancy for five of six analyses (Figure 4; Table S1). For CHNS glucose, discrepancy fell from 0.134019 to 0.024079 HbA1c percentage points per mmol/\allowbreak{}L. All three ENSANUT contrasts improved. CHNS BMI instead worsened, from 0.020380 to 0.031557, showing that additional information did not improve every requested effect.


The individual laws explain the aggregate contrast. CHNS E1 glucose means spanned 0.363363–\allowbreak{}0.414189 around the 0.402093 reference; BMI means were positive but lower than the reference. Conditional-age means ranged from −0.000509 to 0.007393 versus 0.008107, including one negative constructor mean. Every E1 author used at least one relation numerically, yet useful source information did not uniquely determine the target joint law. The prescribed E1 statistical constructions also had lower discrepancies than every corresponding agent for CHNS glucose and ENSANUT conditional BMI; other comparisons favored agent laws. The result supports the value of relational information and inspectable model construction without an overall algorithmic ranking.


\subsection{Supplied summaries enable joint reconstruction}

The summary-assisted study tests a different information condition: three authors received literature alone (L), while three received that literature plus 30 target summaries and their metadata (R). Each delivered three final tables. R closely reconstructed all five highlighted regression and correlation analyses together (Figure S1; Table S2). Constructor-mean BMI coefficients ranged from 0.052821 to 0.055789 around the supplied 0.054946; glucose coefficients ranged from 0.396941 to 0.413715 around 0.402093. Both Pearson and Spearman glucose–\allowbreak{}HbA1c correlations were also close, preserving joint features that differed in several external-evidence constructions.


Agreement extended to spread: R constructor-mean HbA1c SDs were 0.95208–\allowbreak{}0.96554 versus 0.95357. A separate purpose-built statistical calibration supplement likewise closely reconstructed the regression summaries. These results show that available aggregate information can become useful joint data. They measure reconstruction of supplied information and remain separate from Q1's external-evidence comparison.


\subsection{Retrieval and sensitivity to an unreported relationship}

Four open-retrieval authors selected sixteen paper uses representing twelve originals and produced twelve complete CHNS/\allowbreak{}ENSANUT tables (Figure S2; Tables S3–\allowbreak{}S4). All 36 primary points retained their reference directions, while rank and residual-spread discrepancies remained.


The supporting dependence example illustrates another useful outcome of explicit construction. Before generation, two admissible BMI–\allowbreak{}glucose latent correlations, 0.10 and 0.40, implied conditional BMI coefficients +0.03422 and −0.01095 while preserving margins and the secondary projection (Figure S4). Making the law explicit identified an unreported relationship capable of changing the requested conditional conclusion.


\section{Discussion}

Scientific requests and study-qualified partial evidence can become executable joint laws and analyzable virtual cohorts without individual-level construction records. The agent's central role is choosing how evidence enters that law: qualifying study findings, deriving compatible numerical constraints and completing unreported relationships. The table makes those choices usable by ordinary scientific software; the specification makes their consequences inspectable. Established distributions and deterministic numerical tools provide the implementation, while the agent supplies the task-specific scientific model.


The HRS reconstruction shows what happens when a published study supplies the model terms. Both constructions preserved the lower-branch interaction, yet the weaker upper-branch point estimates differed in sign. Under external evidence, a similarly interpretable distinction emerged: an ENSANUT generator could approximate the glucose-adjusted BMI coefficient while understating HbA1c variation, and a Turkana generator could approximate total cholesterol dispersion while understating residual dependence. Each comparison evaluates a specific use of the generated data. More synthetic rows would narrow Monte Carlo variation under the chosen law while leaving the omitted dependence unresolved.


For the present cross-task outputs, supported use is assumption-dependent scenario data for qualitative association exploration and analytical workflow inspection. Quantitative cohort replacement, calibrated design/\allowbreak{}power use and broad population transfer remain unestablished. These are limits of the present evidence, while the general method continues to address whole-cohort generation, enrichment and eventual missing-cell completion. The latter remains unevaluated. The earlier HAALSI evaluation established prospective direction agreement for two main effects, with mixed results for their interaction and an unsuccessful joint decision. Assessing transport of the later evidence-use interface would require a separate, previously unexposed target.


The current studies also leave component-level questions open. All six cross-task authors retained their initial laws, and all 132 runtime source-check entries were unavailable under the semantic rules (Table S6). Numerical evidence still entered reviewed parameter derivations, but this batch demonstrates no numerical source-fit feedback or revision gain. Likewise, the studies do not isolate a benefit of retrieval, review or a particular probability family. Fresh contexts share model services and some sources, and neither seeded draws nor conditional intervals quantify evidence, extraction or structural uncertainty. Human time savings and arbitrary-request autonomy require separate assessment. The strongest current result is a completed, inspectable scientific workflow with observable analytical successes and failures.

\subsection*{S3. Open retrieval across population requests}

The unified workflow allocated two fresh scientific authors to each of the existing CHNS and ENSANUT requests. Each author received the intended analyses, population/\allowbreak{}measurement definitions, task interface and retrieval budget, but no target coefficients, desired signs, individual records or prior construction. CHNS- and ENSANUT-derived studies were excluded as generating evidence. Across 24 query credits, the authors selected sixteen paper uses representing twelve distinct originals. Independent review checked the original statistical definitions, numerical derivations, source applicability and required finite moments before execution. Sources could overlap across authors; four constructions do not imply four independent bodies of clinical evidence.


The agents chose materially different laws. C01 used an age-conditioned multivariate-lognormal mixture informed by Chinese population summaries [7,16] and contextual age/\allowbreak{}body-size findings [10,17]. C02 combined copula predictors with an affine HbA1c conditional mean using Pinggu summaries and Singapore coefficients [18,19], with other populations as applicability context [14,20]. E01 used bounded beta predictors and a conditional gamma response [10,17,21,22]. E02 combined lognormal BMI, conditional truncated-normal glucose and a positive conditional-mean lognormal response [10,21,23,24]. Their unreported dependencies, population transfers and residual choices remained declared assumptions. The shared controller did not prescribe these families or insert target coefficients.

The sixteen physical executions comprised four trials and twelve unique final tables, or 36,000 final virtual rows. Feedback contained 29 qualified approximate comparisons and 39 unavailable checks across the four trials, not source-population replays or 29 passed constraints. After all sessions closed, the evaluator reused the fixed sample references. A separate numerical implementation reproduced all 366 final coefficient/\allowbreak{}diagnostic values; no clinical data were downloaded or refitted.


All 36 primary seed estimates had the same positive directions as their corresponding references (Figure S2). These are repeated measurements of six analysis definitions across four laws, not 36 independent confirmations. Magnitude recovery varied by analysis. \subsection*{S4. Longitudinal reconstruction from published artifacts}

The HRS recipient-stage example [1] tested a different data product. A fresh agent received published model exports, a variable specification and the intended mixed-model analysis. Each generated 11,509 subjects at 0, 2, 4 and 6 years, producing 46,036 rows per table. No HRS individual records were used, and the source analysis coefficients were construction inputs.


All three agent and three corrected-law tables supported the native R/\allowbreak{}lme4 model with 16 fixed effects, individual intercepts/\allowbreak{}slopes and piecewise HbA1c terms around 6.5\%, with age centered at 60 and scaled per decade. The below-threshold HbA1c-by-age interaction averaged −0.044635 in agent data, with range −0.051059 to −0.038247, against the supplied −0.046837. The reconstructed law averaged −0.040500. Both preserved its negative direction in all three draws (Figure 2).

\subsection*{S5. Cross-task construction, dispersion and source uses}

The next study applied the common workflow to HbA1c analyses in the ENSANUT2018 analytical design and BMI-related TC/\allowbreak{}HDL analyses in the Turkana study context. Three fresh agent contexts constructed a law for each request, using reviewed external articles, population/\allowbreak{}measurement metadata and optional literature tools. No participant inputs, target coefficients, target means or prior generators were supplied. The six contexts used the same model service and shared some source evidence. This study therefore tests repeated construction across two requests, with the law chosen by each author, rather than independent clinical replication or a controlled comparison of workflow components.


The HbA1c analyses retained the existing age and BMI estimands and equal allocation to eight sex-by-age cells. The lipid analyses used unweighted age/\allowbreak{}sex-adjusted BMI coefficients for TC and HDL, plus the Pearson correlation of their residuals after age, sex and BMI adjustment. Lipid eligibility required adults with recognized recorded sex and finite, positive BMI and lipid values. Generated lipid concentrations were converted from mmol/\allowbreak{}L to mg/\allowbreak{}dL using the frozen factor 38.67. The reference represents a selected Turkana/\allowbreak{}Laikipia study sample; fasting, assay transfer and population composition remained qualified in the evidence record. No new participant records were read, downloaded or refitted for this study.


The agents made different scientific choices within the common interface. H01 combined bounded predictors, conditionally truncated glucose and a shifted gamma HbA1c innovation; H02 used bounded beta conditional laws. H03 solved cross-source moment equations under declared dependence and dispersion assumptions, then used conditional beta laws. Their source records included overlapping age, body-size and glycemia evidence [10,17,21,22], with additional check-only/\allowbreak{}contextual evidence for H01 [14]. The lipid authors combined Gaussian copulas and lognormal margins using geometric summaries from Greiner et al. [25], rural Ashanti rank correlations [26] and arithmetic moments from Agongo et al. [27,28]. Choices about arithmetic versus geometric location, transfer of coefficients of variation, sex strata and age dependence differed among L01–\allowbreak{}L03. These are applications of established probability families whose numerical use was selected and explained by the agent.


Independent source and implementation reviews checked the statistical meanings, specification/\allowbreak{}code consistency and finite moments before isolated execution. Each constructor completed one scheduled trial, inspected generated diagnostics and source feedback, and could revise once. All six retained their initial laws. Three fixed final seeds, 9101–\allowbreak{}9103, produced 18 distinct 3,000-row tables: 54,000 fictional rows from 24 successful physical attempts, including the six trials. No final execution failed and no seed was replaced. Reference comparison began after all six author sessions closed and reused previously exposed aggregate results.


All 54 primary point estimates had the same direction as their corresponding sample estimates, with quantitative agreement varying by analysis (Figure 3; Table S5). HbA1c age effects averaged 0.09954–\allowbreak{}0.10091 percentage points per decade versus 0.07513 in the reference; age/\allowbreak{}sex-adjusted BMI effects averaged 0.04892–\allowbreak{}0.06030 versus 0.08364 per 5 kg/\allowbreak{}m². With glucose additionally adjusted, H03 averaged 0.05098 versus 0.05664, whereas H01 and H02 averaged 0.03047 and 0.01936. Thus the shared age direction and attenuation after glucose adjustment did not imply recovery of each effect magnitude.


For lipids, mean HDL BMI coefficients ranged from −2.1865 to −1.9868 mg/\allowbreak{}dL per 5 kg/\allowbreak{}m² versus −4.6230 in the reference, while TC coefficients ranged from 10.0151 to 11.2609 versus 5.6831. The TC reference sensitivity interval was −3.7535 to 15.1197, so matching its positive point estimate does not establish replication of a definite positive population effect. Residual TC–\allowbreak{}HDL correlations averaged 0.10828–\allowbreak{}0.14134 versus 0.51207. The generators preserved the directions of these focal point estimates while substantially underrepresenting the lipid dependence remaining after the specified adjustment.


Dispersion changes the assessment of usefulness (Figure S3). H03's closer glucose-adjusted BMI coefficient accompanied lower HbA1c marginal SD and full-model residual RMS, 0.3523 and 0.3006 percentage points versus 0.5274 and 0.4885. H02 retained more variation, with corresponding values 0.4825 and 0.4036, but smaller BMI coefficients. L02's TC SD was close to the reference, 43.6778 versus 42.8466 mg/\allowbreak{}dL, although its residual TC–\allowbreak{}HDL correlation remained weak. All three lipid laws had HDL SDs below the reference. No construction uniformly dominated across focal effects, dependence and dispersion.
